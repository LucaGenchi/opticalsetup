Optical parametric amplifier (OPA)

Set the pump, the crystal and the seed; read the gain, the idler, the pump depletion and the energy balance. The numbers come from the functions OpticalSetup’s parametric amplifier is built on, and are checked against independent solutions of the same equations.

Model
Plane waves, collinear; gain evaluated instant by instant through the pulses (quasi-static); pump depletion as an energy limit, without back-conversion.
Evidence
Reference-checked against independent solutions of the same equations, including an exact solution with pump depletion: validation table. Not compared with a measurement.
Code
parametric.js and parametric-amplifier.js, the functions the canvas’s OPA element is being built on (the element itself is not in the canvas yet).

    The physics

    An optical parametric amplifier transfers energy from a strong pump wave to a weak signal (the seed) inside a crystal without inversion symmetry, which has a second-order (χ⁽²⁾) response [1][7][14]. Each pump photon that disappears creates one signal photon and one idler photon, so the idler frequency is fixed by energy conservation:

    Because photons are created in pairs, the Manley–Rowe relations [1][6] fix how the converted pump power divides. For every watt the signal gains, the idler receives λs/λi watts and the pump loses λs/λp watts; nothing is left in the crystal as heat (apart from absorption, which is not modelled):

    ΔPi=λsλi ΔPs,−ΔPp=λsλp ΔPs=ΔPs+ΔPi\Delta P_i = \frac{\lambda_s}{\lambda_i}\,\Delta P_s, \qquad -\Delta P_p = \frac{\lambda_s}{\lambda_p}\,\Delta P_s = \Delta P_s + \Delta P_iΔPi​=λi​λs​​ΔPs​,−ΔPp​=λp​λs​​ΔPs​=ΔPs​+ΔPi​

    Pulses: gain instant by instant

    A parametric amplifier stores no energy: it amplifies only while the pump is present [14][17][21]. The calculator therefore evaluates the gain separately at each instant of the pulse, with the pump intensity of that instant (Gaussian intensity envelopes, FWHM durations), and averages the result over the seed [13]:

    What d_eff is, and why it is in pm/V

    A strong optical field distorts the electron clouds of a crystal slightly anharmonically. The induced polarization then contains a part proportional to the product of two fields, which radiates at their sum or difference frequency. In a parametric amplifier the pump and the signal together drive a polarization at the idler frequency (and pump and idler one at the signal frequency) [7]:

    P(2)(ωi)  ∝  ε0 deff  E(ωp) E∗(ωs),χ(2)=2dP^{(2)}(\omega_i) \;\propto\; \varepsilon_0\, d_\text{eff}\; E(\omega_p)\, E^{*}(\omega_s), \qquad \chi^{(2)} = 2dP(2)(ωi​)∝ε0​deff​E(ωp​)E∗(ωs​),χ(2)=2d
    The numerical prefactor depends on how the field amplitudes are defined (Boyd, section 2.2 [7]); Γ above already includes it.

    Units. ε0E already has the units of a polarization, so the product d·E must be a pure number: d has the units of an inverse field, metres per volt. The values are around 10⁻¹² m/V, hence picometres per volt. A field of 1 GV/m (an intensity of about 210 GW/cm² in a crystal of index 1.6, from I = ½nε0cE²) times 2 pm/V gives 0.002: the nonlinear response is a small correction to the linear one, and needs intense light to matter.

    “Effective”. A crystal has a tensor of coefficients dij. deff is the single number that remains for your propagation direction and your three polarizations (for birefringent phase matching it depends on the crystal angles). For quasi-phase matching, where the sign of d is flipped every half period, the first-order Fourier factor 2/π applies: deff = (2/π) d33, for ideal poling with a 50 % duty cycle and all three waves polarized along the axis that uses d33 [11].

    CrystalCoefficient measuredTypical deff for an OPA
    β-barium borate (BBO), type Id22 = 2.2 pm/V at 1064 nm (2.16 before rounding) [10]about 2 pm/V (d22 times a factor near 1 that depends on the angles)
    Periodically poled lithium niobate (PPLN)d33 = 25.2 pm/V at 1064 nm [9]about 16 pm/V = (2/π) d33 [11]
    Periodically poled KTP (PPKTP)d33 = 14.6 pm/V at 1064 nm [9]about 9 pm/V = (2/π) d33

    These are orders of magnitude, not design values: the coefficients depend on wavelength (they fall towards the infrared), on the crystal’s composition and on the measurement: Eckardt et al. give about 4 % reproducibility and about 10 % absolute accuracy [10]. Use the deff of your crystal cut from its data sheet or a crystal handbook [8].

    Reading the graphs

    1. Signal gain against seed delay. Each point is the whole calculation repeated with the seed arriving earlier or later. The blue curve includes the energy limit, the green one does not. Their gap shows where the pump is being depleted: the blue curve flattens where the seed takes all the pump energy it meets. The gain falls much faster than the pulses’ overlap, because the exponent follows the pump intensity. Shown only when both pump and seed are pulsed.

    2. Pump conversion against crystal length. The blue curve is the model the app uses: undepleted gain limited by the pump energy in each instant. The orange curve is an exact reference for plane waves: the same pulses, timing and seed, cut into time slices, but in each slice the coupled equations are solved with pump depletion instead of the energy limit; the note under the graph says how well its time average has converged. Where the two agree, the limit is a good approximation. Past the optimum length they separate: a real plane wave converts energy back into the pump (back-conversion), so the orange curve peaks and then falls, while the model stays at its limit. A single plane wave would fall to zero and rise again periodically; the orange curve is smoother because each instant of the pulse reaches its optimum at a different length. In a real beam, the transverse profile smooths it further [15][13]. The orange curve ignores ηmax and is drawn for one seed only.

    3. Signal gain against peak pump intensity. The whole calculation repeated at other pump intensities, with and without the energy limit. At low intensity both follow the exponential growth; where they separate, the pump starts to be depleted and the gain saturates.

    Hover or tap a graph to read its values; each graph also has a table of its data.

    Physical accuracy: a guide to trends and orders of magnitude

    Manzoni & Cerullo describe the undepleted, monochromatic plane-wave calculation as giving “the upper limit for the parametric gain” [17]; the peak small-signal gain here is that quantity, under the assumptions stated above. Real amplifiers fall below it because:

    • only the centre of a real beam sees the peak intensity, and the beam profile changes during amplification (gain guiding) [15];
    • signal, idler and pump travel at different group velocities and separate after the pulse-splitting length, which ends the gain for short pulses [17][14];
    • birefringent walk-off separates the beams in space, and dispersion inside the crystal chirps the pulses;
    • absorption of the idler changes the energy transfer: it reduces the small-signal gain, while in pulsed oscillators it can even raise the efficiency by suppressing back-conversion [18]; either way it needs a model with losses;
    • a real crystal is never exactly phase matched over the whole seed spectrum.

    The gain is also very sensitive to its inputs. At high gain G ≈ ¼e2ΓL. At ΓL = 7, a 10 % error in deff (the absolute accuracy of the measured coefficients [10]) changes the small-signal gain by a factor of about 4.1, and a 20 % error in the intensity by about 3.8. Read the page for orders of magnitude, trends, the idler wavelength, the energy balance and the timing tolerance; for design, use a model that includes the beams’ transverse profile and the pulses’ propagation [13][15].

    What is not modelled

    • Transverse beam profile, diffraction, gain guiding and spatial walk-off: all waves are plane waves at the peak intensity [15].
    • Group-velocity mismatch, group-velocity dispersion and chirp inside the crystal: each instant of the pulse is amplified independently [17].
    • Noncollinear geometry (NOPA) and its broadband phase matching [19][14]; the phase mismatch is a number you enter, not computed from dispersion data.
    • Parametric noise: an unseeded crystal gives nothing here, while a real one produces parametric fluorescence (optical parametric generation) [5].
    • The degenerate case λs = 2λp with signal and idler in one mode, which is phase-sensitive [21]; the calculator declines it.
    • A seeded idler, absorption (of any wave), thermal effects and crystal damage.
    • Back-conversion in the model itself: only the orange reference curve shows it, for plane waves.
