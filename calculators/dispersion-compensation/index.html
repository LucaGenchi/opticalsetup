Dispersion compensation

Choose a prism or grating layout, the pulse and the chirp it carries; the page solves the separation (or takes yours), draws the bench to scale with the canvas’s own tracer, and gives the compensator’s GDD, TOD and FOD, the output pulse from every order of the phase, the colours’ spread and the transmission.

    Model
    Exact plane-wave phase of each layout along the central ray: Fork, Martinez and Gordon’s apex path for prisms, Treacy’s for gratings, Martinez’s equivalent pair for the stretcher (paraxial). The output pulse is the Fourier transform of the spectrum with every order of the phase.

    The physics

    A pulse’s spectral phase φ(ω), expanded about its central frequency ω₀, sets its shape in time. The linear term is a delay; the quadratic one, the group delay dispersion (GDD), is a linear chirp; the third- and fourth-order terms (TOD and FOD) distort the pulse further. Glass, lenses and fibres add positive GDD at visible and near-infrared wavelengths. A compensator adds the opposite.

    Prism pair. Each colour leaves the first prism in its own direction u(ω) and is made parallel again by the second, identical prism, turned the other way. Fork, Martinez and Gordon showed that the phase between a wavefront before the first prism and one after the second equals that of a virtual ray from apex A of the first to the wavefront through apex B of the second [1]: no glass on that ray, only the projection of the apex-to-apex vector on the colour’s direction. Moving a prism into the beam moves its apex, which is how insertion adds glass. The page evaluates this exactly, with the colour’s direction from Snell’s law at both faces:

    φ(ω)=ωc (B−A)⋅u(ω),δ(ω)=θ1+arcsin⁡ ⁣(nsin⁡(α−arcsin⁡(sin⁡θ1/n)))−α\varphi(\omega)=\frac{\omega}{c}\,(\mathbf B-\mathbf A)\cdot\mathbf u(\omega),\qquad \delta(\omega)=\theta_1+\arcsin\!\big(n\sin(\alpha-\arcsin(\sin\theta_1/n))\big)-\alphaφ(ω)=cω​(B−A)⋅u(ω),δ(ω)=θ1​+arcsin(nsin(α−arcsin(sinθ1​/n)))−α
    u turns from the input direction by the deviation δ, with n = n(ω) the glass’s index, α the apex angle and θ₁ the angle of incidence. A Brewster-cut prism at minimum deviation has tan θ₁ = n and α = π − 2θ₁ at the central wavelength.

    Grating pair. Treacy’s compressor [2]: two parallel gratings a perpendicular distance G apart, used in order −1. In the plane-wave form used here the phase is

    φ(ω)=ωc Gcos⁡β(ω),sin⁡β=λd−sin⁡γ,GDD=−λ3G2πc2d2cos⁡3β\varphi(\omega)=\frac{\omega}{c}\,G\cos\beta(\omega),\qquad \sin\beta=\frac{\lambda}{d}-\sin\gamma,\qquad \mathrm{GDD}=-\frac{\lambda^3 G}{2\pi c^2 d^2\cos^3\beta}φ(ω)=cω​Gcosβ(ω),sinβ=dλ​−sinγ,GDD=−2πc2d2cos3βλ3G​
    γ is the angle of incidence, β the diffraction angle, d the groove spacing. The GDD is Treacy’s explicit form, which the page’s derivative reproduces.

    Grating stretcher. Two gratings with two lenses of focal length f between them, 2f apart, each grating a distance s from its lens: the lenses image one grating onto the other, and the pair acts like a grating pair of separation (s₁ + s₂ − 2f) cos β [3]. With the gratings inside the focal planes that is negative, and the GDD positive: the stretcher of a chirped-pulse amplifier [4][5]. This holds to paraxial order in ideal thin lenses; the canvas traces the lenses exactly, which differs from it by 0.14 % on the default bench.

    Geq=(s1+s2−2f)cos⁡β0G_\text{eq}=(s_1+s_2-2f)\cos\beta_0Geq​=(s1​+s2​−2f)cosβ0​

    Double pass and sequences. Sending the light back through the same pair, or through a mirror-image second pair, doubles the phase and makes the colours coincide again: a single pass leaves them side by side (spatial chirp). The page draws the double pass with a roof mirror in the plane of the figure, set beside the beam, and a D-shaped mirror that picks the return off beside the input. A parallel shift of the beam does not change a pair’s phase (it depends only on the prisms’ or gratings’ positions), so the GDD is exactly twice one pass. An in-plane roof does reverse the colours across the beam, so in the figure the spatial chirp doubles; in the lab the roof is turned out of the plane and the return passes below the input, with no spatial chirp. A four-element sequence is two pairs, the second the mirror image of the first; between the pairs the colours travel parallel, so the gap does not change the phase.

    Derivatives and the solve. The phase is evaluated in Taylor arithmetic to third order in ω, so GDD and TOD are exact up to rounding; FOD comes from fourth differences of the exact phase. Each layout’s phase is linear in its separation (L, G or s), so the separation that brings the net GDD to the target follows exactly from two evaluations.

    The output pulse. The spectrum (Gaussian or sech² intensity with the FWHM entered) carries the input phase plus the compensator’s, every order; the pulse is its Fourier transform and the duration the FWHM of |E(t)|². The input phase is either a material’s, from its published dispersion fit (the material calculator’s data), or the GDD, TOD and FOD entered. The GDD-only duration, for comparison, is the analytic one:

    Gaussian; sech² uses its own analytic form, as the canvas does. τ₀ is the transform limit, 0.441 λ²/(cΔλ) for a Gaussian and 0.315 λ²/(cΔλ) for sech².

    Spread and transmission. The colours of the FWHM band arrive at the second prism a distance L |δ(λ₁) − δ(λ₂)| apart, and at the second grating G |tan β₁ − tan β₂| apart; with the beam diameter that sets the aperture the second optic needs. Uncoated prism faces transmit by Fresnel’s formulas for the polarization chosen, at each face’s angle (see prism Fresnel); at Brewster’s angle p-polarized light crosses without loss. Gratings transmit the efficiency entered once per diffraction.

    Choosing a layout

    The defaults show the trade-offs; each number below is the page’s own for that bench.

    • Prism pairs are weak but clean. The fused-silica pair adds −2.2 fs² per mm of separation in double pass at 800 nm, so cancelling 10 mm of N-BK7 takes about 70 cm. A flint (the four N-SF10 prisms) adds −18 fs² per mm. Brewster-cut and p-polarized, the prisms lose nothing to reflection. Sliding the second prism in or out tunes the GDD continuously without moving the beam.
    • Prisms take back part of the glass’s TOD. Glass adds positive GDD and positive TOD; a prism pair adds negative GDD with negative TOD, so it removes part of the glass’s TOD with its GDD. A strongly dispersive flint removes more TOD than the glass brought: on the four-prism default the output (34.8 fs) stays above the transform limit (31.4 fs) because of the TOD left over.
    • Grating pairs are strong. The 1200 lines/mm pair at 45° adds about −2900 fs² per mm of separation in double pass, enough to recompress a pulse stretched to 88 ps in 35 cm. Its TOD is positive with negative GDD: matched to a grating stretcher of the same gratings and angle, it cancels the stretcher’s TOD and FOD too, which is how chirped-pulse amplifiers recompress [4][5]. Each diffraction loses a little (92 % per bounce on the defaults: 72 % for four bounces).
    • Gratings and glass add TOD of the same sign. On the fibre default (20 m of fused silica at 1030 nm, four gratings) the GDD cancels but the TOD adds up, and the output (205 fs) stays well above the transform limit (139 fs). Prisms, or a combination of prisms and gratings, are the usual remedy; this page models each separately.
    • Near Littrow the gratings are most efficient but the beams overlap. In the plane of the figure the second grating would block the input; real compressors separate the beams in height. The page draws the bench further from the source where it can, and says so where no in-plane layout works. The numbers do not depend on the drawing.

    Reading the graphs

    1. GDD across the spectrum. The compensator’s GDD (blue), the input’s (dashed) and the net (green) from 1.5 bandwidths below to 1.5 above the centre; the yellow band is the FWHM. At the centre the net is the target. A sloped net curve is residual TOD, a curved one residual FOD.

    2. Output duration against the separation. The bench recomputed at other separations around the one shown. The minimum of the solid curve is the shortest pulse this layout gives; with TOD left over it lies slightly away from zero net GDD, where the dashed GDD-only curve has its minimum. Far from it only the dashed curve is drawn, because the pulse is long.

    3. The output pulse. Its intensity in time, peak 1, against the transform limit. Satellites on one side are the mark of TOD.

    Physical accuracy

    The phase is exact for plane waves and ideal optics: flat faces, perfect gratings, the glass’s published index. Real compressors depart from it through the beam’s finite size (each part of the beam crosses a different amount of glass), surface and grating errors, misalignment (a prism pair whose faces are not exactly parallel leaves angular dispersion) and the gratings’ groove density tolerance. The page is a design aid for the geometry and the orders of the phase; align and measure the real one.

    What is not modelled

    • Beam size and diffraction: everything is along the central ray of each colour.
    • Grisms, chirped mirrors, and prisms combined with gratings in one compressor.
    • Grating efficiency against wavelength, angle and polarization: one number per diffraction.
    • Absorption in the glass, coatings, mirror losses, the D-shaped mirror’s edge.
    • Thick or real lenses in the stretcher: ideal thin lenses, to paraxial order in the page’s numbers.
    • Nonlinear effects in the material carrying the chirp (self-phase modulation in a fibre, for instance).
