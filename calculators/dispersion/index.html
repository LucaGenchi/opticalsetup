Material dispersion

Set the laser’s central wavelength and bandwidth, then a material and thickness. Read the input pulse, the material’s phase and group index, group-velocity and third-order dispersion, and what the thickness does to the pulse: GDD, TOD, delay and stretched duration.

    Model
    Published room-temperature dispersion fits with analytic derivatives to third order. No absorption, temperature or stress correction.
    Evidence
    Equations checked against independent Python references, with published or exact algebraic anchors. These checks do not establish experimental accuracy.
    Code
    The calculator’s material module; the workbench glasses (every SCHOTT glass here, and fused silica) reuse the canvas catalogue’s coefficients.

    The dispersion model

    Each material is described by its source’s dispersion formula, with λ in micrometres. Solids and water use the Sellmeier form, C in µm² and an optional constant A; air uses Ciddor’s form for n − 1 [12]. The input fields take vacuum wavelengths in nm.

    n2(λ)−1=A+∑jBjλ2λ2−Cj,nair(λ)−1=∑jBjCj−λ−2n^2(\lambda)-1=A+\sum_j\frac{B_j\lambda^2}{\lambda^2-C_j},\qquad n_{\text{air}}(\lambda)-1=\sum_j\frac{B_j}{C_j-\lambda^{-2}}n2(λ)−1=A+j∑​λ2−Cj​Bj​λ2​,nair​(λ)−1=j∑​Cj​−λ−2Bj​​

    The group index describes how the envelope travels. GVD and TOD are the second and third derivatives of the propagation constant k = nω/c with respect to angular frequency, written here in wavelength derivatives. The page evaluates the derivatives analytically and converts β₂ to fs²/mm and β₃ to fs³/mm.

    β3=d3kdω3=−λ44π2c3(3d2ndλ2+λd3ndλ3)\beta_3=\frac{d^3k}{d\omega^3}=-\frac{\lambda^4}{4\pi^2c^3}\left(3\frac{d^2n}{d\lambda^2}+\lambda\frac{d^3n}{d\lambda^3}\right)β3​=dω3d3k​=−4π2c3λ4​(3dλ2d2n​+λdλ3d3n​)
    GDD=Lβ2,TOD=Lβ3\mathrm{GDD}=L\beta_2,\qquad \mathrm{TOD}=L\beta_3GDD=Lβ2​,TOD=Lβ3​
    L in mm × β in fs²/mm or fs³/mm gives fs² or fs³.

    The laser bandwidth

    The bandwidth Δλ is the spectral FWHM. The delay spread is the group-delay difference between the two band edges, taken from the group index at each edge rather than from GDD × Δω, so it stays exact for the material curve over a broad band. A positive value means the blue edge arrives last.

    Δτ=Lc[ng ⁣(λ0−Δλ2)−ng ⁣(λ0+Δλ2)]\Delta\tau=\frac{L}{c}\left[n_g\!\left(\lambda_0-\tfrac{\Delta\lambda}{2}\right)-n_g\!\left(\lambda_0+\tfrac{\Delta\lambda}{2}\right)\right]Δτ=cL​[ng​(λ0​−2Δλ​)−ng​(λ0​+2Δλ​)]

    The input pulse is assumed transform-limited. Its duration comes from the time–bandwidth product K (0.441 for Gaussian, 0.315 for sech² pulses) in the small-bandwidth form used throughout the workbench. A Gaussian pulse stretched by GDD has the closed form below. For sech² pulses the page uses the workbench’s converged Fourier-propagation table. Both include GDD only: TOD is listed but not included in the output duration.

    τ0=Kλ02c Δλ,τGauss=τ01+(4ln⁡2  GDDτ02)2\tau_0=K\frac{\lambda_0^2}{c\,\Delta\lambda},\qquad \tau_{\text{Gauss}}=\tau_0\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{4\ln2\;\mathrm{GDD}}{\tau_0^2}\right)^2}τ0​=KcΔλλ02​​,τGauss​=τ0​1+(τ02​4ln2GDD​)2​

    Fused silica and all eight SCHOTT glasses are the canvas catalogue’s own entries, so this page and the tracer evaluate the same curves. The ordinary and extraordinary entries of sapphire, quartz and BBO are principal indices: they hold for light polarized perpendicular or parallel to the optic axis. A beam at another angle in a uniaxial crystal sees an angle-dependent extraordinary index, which is not modelled.

    Precision and limits

    An independent Python reference evaluates each source formula in its own published form, in 50-digit decimal arithmetic, and differentiates k(ω) = nω/c numerically in angular frequency. That route differs from the analytic wavelength derivatives used here. n, ng, GVD and TOD agree to 10⁻⁹ relative, or 10⁻⁹ fs²/mm and fs³/mm absolute near a zero crossing. The catalogue’s published nd and Abbe numbers separately anchor every SCHOTT glass. The four canvas glasses are also checked against the tracer’s own getters.

    The digits describe the fit, not a real sample. Fits carry their own residuals (Malitson quotes about 10⁻⁵ in index for fused silica), and batch, temperature and stress move real indices further. The SCHOTT catalogue gives indices relative to air at its reference conditions; the water and air fits are absolute; for fused silica and the crystals the sources as consulted do not say, and the materials table records which applies. The page applies no air-to-vacuum correction. Converting to absolute indices (n × nair for standard air) raises n by about 3 × 10⁻⁴ × n and also changes the GVD. For N-BK7, for example, it adds 0.19 fs²/mm at 300 nm, 0.045 fs²/mm at 800 nm (0.1 %), 0.020 fs²/mm at 1300 nm (1 %) and 0.003 fs²/mm at 1690 nm. Near a zero-GVD wavelength the relative change has no bound: fused silica’s zero moves by about 0.2 nm. The added group delay L(ng − 1)/c and optical path L(n − 1) are excesses over the same path in the medium the fit is relative to, and the page labels them accordingly: air for the SCHOTT glasses, vacuum for water and air, and “the fit’s reference medium” where that is not stated. Against air instead of vacuum the readings differ by L(ng,air − 1)/c and L(nair − 1), under 0.1 % for the solids here.

    The pulse rows describe a transform-limited input with the stated shape. They do not model a measured spectrum, spectral phase already on the pulse, nonlinear propagation or TOD-induced reshaping. Graphs sample 241 wavelengths; hover, use the arrow keys or open the data tables to read them.
