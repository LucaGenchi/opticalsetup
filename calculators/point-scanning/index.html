Point-scanning resolution and field of view

Estimate the lateral and axial resolution of a point-scanning microscope from the excitation wavelength, the objective’s NA and the detection: a confocal pinhole for fluorescence, spontaneous Raman or scattered light, or two- and three-photon excitation without one. A second panel gives the field of view, how far the focus moves for a galvo angle, the pixel size and how the beam fills the objective.

      The scanned focus

      A point-scanning microscope focuses a laser to a spot, moves the spot over the sample and records one signal per position. The image of a point object is therefore the spot itself, weighted by what the detector accepts [1][4]. For a uniformly filled circular pupil, the scalar intensity near the focus depends on two dimensionless optical coordinates, one across and one along the axis [2][5]:

      Without a pinhole a one-photon signal is proportional to the excitation intensity at the object, so the point image is h at the excitation wavelength. Its FWHM is 0.5145 λexc/NA across and 0.8859 λexc/(n − √(n² − NA²)) along the axis, the values the Zeiss monograph rounds to 0.51 and 0.88 (its eqs. 2 and 3) [1]. For a small NA the axial expression tends to 1.77 nλ/NA² (its eq. 2a). The page finds each FWHM by bisection on the computed profile and converts optical units to micrometres:

      FWHMlat=2v1/22π λexcNA,FWHMax=2u1/24π λexcn−n2−NA2\mathrm{FWHM}_{\text{lat}}=\frac{2v_{1/2}}{2\pi}\,\frac{\lambda_{\text{exc}}}{\mathrm{NA}},\qquad \mathrm{FWHM}_{\text{ax}}=\frac{2u_{1/2}}{4\pi}\,\frac{\lambda_{\text{exc}}}{n-\sqrt{n^2-\mathrm{NA}^2}}FWHMlat​=2π2v1/2​​NAλexc​​,FWHMax​=4π2u1/2​​n−n2−NA2​λexc​​

      The FWHM is not the same as the two-point resolution: the Rayleigh criterion places two incoherent points at the Airy pattern’s first zero, 0.61 λ/NA apart. Without a pinhole, a one-photon scanner has no optical sectioning: every plane of a uniform fluorescent layer returns the same total signal, so the axial FWHM describes isolated points only [2].

      The confocal pinhole

      A pinhole in a plane conjugate to the focus accepts light from a small region around it. The total point image is the product of the illumination and detection point images (Zeiss eq. 1) [1], and the detection point image is the detected-wavelength PSF summed over the back-projected pinhole of radius R [3]:

      PSFtot(r,z)=hexc(r,z)⋅D(r,z),D(r,z)=∬∣s∣≤Rhdet(∣r−s∣, z) d2s\mathrm{PSF}_{\text{tot}}(r,z)=h_{\text{exc}}(r,z)\cdot D(r,z),\qquad D(r,z)=\iint_{|\mathbf s|\le R} h_{\text{det}}\big(|\mathbf r-\mathbf s|,\,z\big)\,d^2sPSFtot​(r,z)=hexc​(r,z)⋅D(r,z),D(r,z)=∬∣s∣≤R​hdet​(∣r−s∣,z)d2s

      The Zeiss monograph defines 1 AU as the Airy-disc diameter [1]; this page evaluates it at the detected wavelength, the light the pinhole passes.

      The two coordinates of hdet scale with λexc/λdet, so a Stokes shift widens the detection side. The page evaluates D numerically for any pinhole between 0 and 10 AU. In the two limits it reproduces the monograph’s closed forms, listed beside the result for comparison [1]:

      • Large pinhole (PH > 1 AU), “geometric-optical confocality”: the resolution is that of the excitation spot alone (eqs. 2, 3). The pinhole still rejects light from other planes; the monograph measures that by the optical slice thickness, the axial FWHM of the detection response D (eq. 4).
      • Point pinhole (PH < 0.25 AU), “wave-optical confocality”: the total PSF is hexc·hdet, which the monograph approximates with one mean wavelength λ̄ (eqs. 6–8). Below 1 AU it no longer distinguishes the optical slice thickness from the axial resolution: the axial FWHM of the total PSF is both.

      Zeiss eqs. 6, 8 and 7. With λdet = λexc the exact values are 0.3693 and 0.6378.

      Resolution and optical sectioning are different properties [4]. At 1 AU the computed lateral width is within a few per cent of the open-detector value, yet the pinhole already rejects light from other planes; the lateral gain appears only well below 1 AU, where the detected signal falls steeply [1]. The monograph recommends z steps of half the slice thickness.

      Spontaneous Raman is a linear, incoherent signal like fluorescence; the detected wavelength is the Stokes line of the chosen shift:

      1λdet=1λexc−Δν~107(λ in nm, Δν~ in cm−1)\frac{1}{\lambda_{\text{det}}}=\frac{1}{\lambda_{\text{exc}}}-\frac{\Delta\tilde\nu}{10^7}\qquad(\lambda\ \text{in nm},\ \Delta\tilde\nu\ \text{in cm}^{-1})λdet​1​=λexc​1​−107Δν~​(λ in nm, Δν~ in cm−1)

      Scattered or reflected light is treated as coming from point-like incoherent scatterers. Extended reflecting structures image coherently: the monograph notes, for example, a factor 0.45 instead of 0.64 in eq. 7 for a mirror [1]. In confocal Raman and reflection work an index mismatch between immersion and sample can make the real depth resolution much worse than these figures [11].

      Two- and three-photon excitation

      An N-photon signal grows with the N-th power of the excitation intensity, so the point image is the excitation focus raised to that power [2][6][7]. Detection needs no pinhole: a large, non-descanned detector collects everything.

      PSFN(u,v)=hexc(u,v)N\mathrm{PSF}_N(u,v)=h_{\text{exc}}(u,v)^NPSFN​(u,v)=hexc​(u,v)N
      So et al. write the two-photon case as I²(u/2, v/2), in the optical units of half the excitation wavelength [2].

      Raising the profile to a power narrows it: at the same wavelength the two-photon point image is about 1.39 times narrower than the one-photon one (0.3693 against 0.5145 λ/NA), the three-photon image about 1.70 times. A two-photon process is, however, excited at roughly twice the wavelength a one-photon process needs for the same transition, so it is wider than one-photon excitation at λ/N; the page lists both comparisons [2][6]. Horton et al. used three-photon excitation at 1700 nm to image deep in the scattering mouse brain [8].

      What multiphoton excitation gains is sectioning without a pinhole: the total signal from a uniform plane falls away from the focus instead of staying constant [2]. That plane response sums hexcN over each plane, so its width is a different quantity from the axial FWHM of the point image, which is what the page reports. Second- and third-harmonic generation from a point-like object have the same point image; extended objects add coherent effects (phase matching, the Gouy phase) that are not modelled.

      Field of view and galvo scanning

      An infinity-corrected objective’s magnification is defined with its maker’s tube lens: 200 mm for Nikon and Leica, 180 mm for Olympus and 165 mm for Zeiss [13]. The objective’s focal length follows from it, and the field of view in the sample is the field number divided by the magnification [14]:

      fobj=frefM,FOV=FNMf_{\text{obj}}=\frac{f_{\text{ref}}}{M},\qquad \mathrm{FOV}=\frac{\mathrm{FN}}{M}fobj​=Mfref​​,FOV=MFN​

      In a laser-scanning microscope a galvanometer mirror tilts the beam, a scan lens turns the tilt into a position in an intermediate image, and the tube lens and the objective image that plane onto the sample [12]. A mirror turned by θmech deflects the beam by twice that angle. An ideal, distortion-free scan lens places the beam at fSL tan θ; an f-theta lens at fSL θ [12]:

      θopt=2 θmech,h=fSLtan⁡θopt  or  fSL θopt\theta_{\text{opt}}=2\,\theta_{\text{mech}},\qquad h=f_{\text{SL}}\tan\theta_{\text{opt}}\ \ \text{or}\ \ f_{\text{SL}}\,\theta_{\text{opt}}θopt​=2θmech​,h=fSL​tanθopt​  or  fSL​θopt​

      x is the displacement of the focus in the sample and θpupil the beam angle at the back aperture. The field width is twice x at half the peak-to-peak range.

      Scan and tube lens form a telescope that images the galvo onto the objective’s back aperture and expands the beam by fTL/fSL [12]. The resolution panel assumes a uniformly filled aperture. The Zeiss monograph describes the fill by the truncation ratio T of the 1/e² beam diameter to the pupil diameter: below about 0.6 the focus is Gaussian, above about 1 it is close to the Airy pattern, and beyond 3 the resolution no longer improves while more power is clipped [1].

      The sampling theorem applied to the point image: fc is the highest spatial frequency it passes, the cutoff of its transfer function. A product of PSFs has the convolution of their transfer functions, so the cutoffs add; the PSF width and the cutoff are different measures [7]. Near fc the contrast is weak, and with a large pinhole the detection part adds little, so the FWHM rule is the usual practical choice.

      Precision and limits

      Numerics. Bessel functions use rational approximations accurate to about 10⁻⁸. The pinhole integral uses Gauss–Legendre quadrature (64 radial × 48 angular nodes across the pinhole, 128 nodes over the pupil for the defocused field), and each FWHM is bisected to round-off. An independent Python reference, using integral representations of the Bessel functions and a different quadrature, agrees with the page to 10⁻⁶ relative or better in every case of the validation table (the test tolerance is 10⁻⁵); the open and point-pinhole limits are also checked against their analytic half-maximum points.

      Scope. Scalar optics with a uniformly filled, aberration-free circular pupil; the axial coordinate uses n(1 − cos α), exact on the axis, and the defocused field off the axis is the paraxial form. Above about NA/n = 0.7 the vector nature of the focused field (Richards and Wolf [9]) widens the real focus along the polarization, which this page does not include. Emission is taken at a single wavelength; a broad band widens the detection side slightly. The sample is assumed index-matched to the immersion medium, non-scattering and free of aberrations; real foci deep in tissue or through a mismatched index are wider, and the depth scale itself changes [11]. Cole et al. describe how to measure the point image with sub-resolution beads and compare it with such limits [10].

      Scanning. Thin, ideal scan and tube lenses in a telescope that images the galvo onto the back aperture; an objective that maps the pupil angle to fobj tan θ; no distortion, field curvature or vignetting. The field number limits where the objective is corrected; the page flags a scanned field beyond it but does not model the loss.
