      The fiber mode

      A single-mode fiber guides one field pattern, its fundamental mode LP₀₁. For a step-index fiber with core radius a and numerical aperture NA, the mode depends on one number, V [1]:

      V=2πa NAλ,single mode for V<2.405,λc=2πa NA2.405V = \frac{2\pi a\,\mathrm{NA}}{\lambda},\qquad \text{single mode for } V < 2.405,\qquad \lambda_c = \frac{2\pi a\,\mathrm{NA}}{2.405}V=λ2πaNA​,single mode for V<2.405,λc​=2.4052πaNA​
      2.405 is the first zero of the Bessel function J₀; above it the next mode, LP₁₁, is guided too. λ_c is the cutoff wavelength.

      The exact mode is a Bessel function in the core and a decaying one in the cladding, but it is very nearly Gaussian [2]. Marcuse fitted the radius wm of the Gaussian that matches it best:

      wma=0.65+1.619V3/2+2.879V6,MFD=2wm\frac{w_m}{a} = 0.65 + \frac{1.619}{V^{3/2}} + \frac{2.879}{V^6},\qquad \mathrm{MFD} = 2w_mawm​​=0.65+V3/21.619​+V62.879​,MFD=2wm​

      The independent reference solves the exact mode and finds that this fit stays within 1 % of the best Gaussian for 1.2 ≤ V ≤ 3.5, so the page accepts that range and asks for the mode-field diameter otherwise. The best Gaussian couples to the exact mode at 97.7 % for V = 1.6 and 99.7 % for V = 2.4: the Gaussian model overstates the coupling into a real fiber by that margin. A fiber with an 8.2 µm core and NA 0.14 has V = 2.33 at 1550 nm, a cutoff at 1500 nm and a 9.22 µm mode by the fit. Real fibers are not ideal step-index profiles, and their datasheets quote a measured mode-field diameter (usually by the Petermann II definition) that can differ from the fit by more than its 1 %. When you have the datasheet value, use it; the page’s example takes 10.4 µm.

      Choosing the lens

      The lens should focus the beam to the size of the mode. A collimated beam of radius w at the lens focuses to w′ = w f/√(f² + zR²), where zR is the input beam’s Rayleigh range [5], so the lens that matches the mode exactly is

      f=wm zRw2−wm2≈πw wmλf = \frac{w_m\,z_R}{\sqrt{w^2-w_m^2}} \approx \frac{\pi w\,w_m}{\lambda}f=w2−wm2​​wm​zR​​≈λπwwm​​

      For the example, a 2 mm beam at 1550 nm onto a 10.4 µm mode, that is 10.54 mm. The match is forgiving: a waist 10 % off still couples 99.1 %, 25 % off 95.2 %, 50 % off 85.2 %. Mode matching stays at 95 % or more for any focal length from 8.395 to 13.23 mm, so a catalogue lens in that range does nearly as well: the 11 mm lens the second part of the page starts from gives a 10.85 µm spot, and 99.8 %. The focused waist sits slightly short of f (here 10.9997 mm), which matters only for slow beams.

      A real lens is not ideal. The simplest one, a plano-convex singlet, brings its outer rays to a focus short of its inner ones (spherical aberration), which spreads the spot. The page suggests one at the matching focal length and traces it with the Thin and thick lens calculator’s engine, the fiber at its best position on the axis: its curved side toward the collimated beam, the way round with less spherical aberration; in N-BK7 from 350 to 2000 nm, UV fused silica below and CaF₂ above, the glasses such lenses are usually made of; on the smallest common diameter at least twice the beam’s. For the example it is a 6 mm N-BK7 lens with a 5.277 mm radius, 1.936 mm thick at the centre. Its focus has a Strehl ratio of 0.667, and with the fiber 82.6 µm nearer the lens than F′ it couples 90.6 %; flat side first it would couple 67.1 %. The aberration grows with the lens’s size at the same focusing angle: a 1 mm beam with f = 5.27 mm reaches the same mode at the same angle and couples 96.2 %, while a 5 mm beam at 1064 nm onto a 6.2 µm mode, f = 22.88 mm, couples only 60.5 %. An aspheric lens or an achromatic doublet of the same focal length removes most of this loss: the suggestion is a lens to start from, not the best one.

      Coupling efficiency

      The power coupled into the mode is the overlap of the two fields on the fiber face [3][4]. For two Gaussians whose waists coincide on the face it depends only on the ratio of their radii:

      A lateral offset d, a tilt θ and a focus error Δz each lower it. Here d is where the beam’s centre meets the fiber face, Δz how far the face lies past the focused waist, and θ the angle between the beam and the fiber axis in the plane of the offset, positive when the beam travels away from the axis on the offset side. With an offset and a focus error together, the sign of the tilt matters: with the face past the focus, a beam heading away from the axis couples better, because its focus lay nearer the axis; with the face before the focus, one heading back toward it. For matched waists (w′ = wm = w) and one error at a time the losses take simple forms:

      ηd=e−d2/w2,ηθ=e−(πwθ/λ)2,ηΔz=11+(Δz/2zR)2\eta_d = e^{-d^2/w^2},\qquad \eta_\theta = e^{-(\pi w\theta/\lambda)^2},\qquad \eta_{\Delta z} = \frac{1}{1+\left(\Delta z/2z_R\right)^2}ηd​=e−d2/w2,ηθ​=e−(πwθ/λ)2,ηΔz​=1+(Δz/2zR​)21​
      The page evaluates the general overlap in closed form, with all three errors and any waist ratio at once.

      Lateral offset is the critical one: the example loses 1 dB (21 % of the light) at a 2.55 µm offset, against 58 µm of focus error and 44.6 mrad of tilt. Offset and focus tolerances grow with the mode size and the tilt tolerance shrinks with it: a larger mode is easier to hit and harder to aim.

      Precision and limits

      An independent Python reference integrates the overlap numerically on the fiber face, focuses the beam with the ABCD law, finds the matching focal length by bisection and computes the exact LP₀₁ mode from its Bessel-function equation. The page agrees with it to 10⁻⁹ relative for a given mode-field diameter, and within the fit’s 1 % (2 % in the aligned efficiency) when the mode comes from core and NA.

      Not included: Fresnel reflection at the fiber face (about 3.4 % for uncoated silica, 0.15 dB; an anti-reflection coating or index matching removes most of it), the aberrations of any lens but the suggested plano-convex one, clipping by the lens or by a connector, polarization, beams with M² > 1, and the slight non-Gaussian shape of the true mode. The page refuses a focus steeper than 0.3 rad (half angle) and a fiber face placed before the lens. Its efficiency is the overlap of ideal Gaussian fields, not a guaranteed upper bound: a real fiber’s mode can couple slightly more or less, especially far from alignment.

      The plano-convex suggestion is the Thin and thick lens calculator’s model, with its precision and limits: the scalar focus of the traced singlet on axis, a Gaussian mode taken on the sphere about the fiber’s core. Its glass, diameter and edge thickness (1 mm per 12.7 mm of diameter, at least 1 mm) are rules of thumb for a catalogue lens, not an optimum, and its focal length is the ideal lens’s: a plano-convex lens slightly longer can couple a little more, as its aberrated spot is wider. Expect a catalogue lens to differ in focal length, thickness and coating.
