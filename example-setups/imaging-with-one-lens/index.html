Imaging with one lens

An object 150 mm before a lens of 100 mm focal length: the rays from its tip meet again 300 mm behind the lens, in an image twice the size and upside down.

Background

A lens bends every ray from one point of an object so that they meet again at one point: the image of that point. For a thin lens, the distance s from the object to the lens and the distance s′ from the lens to the image obey 1/s + 1/s′ = 1/f, where f is the focal length, positive for a converging lens[1]. The image is m = −s′/s times the size of the object. A negative m means the image is upside down[1].

Three cases follow from that one equation. With the object further away than f, the rays converge behind the lens to a real image, which a screen or a sensor placed there shows[1]. With the object exactly at f, the rays leave parallel and the image is at infinity[1]. With the object closer than f, the rays still diverge after the lens, as if they came from a larger, upright image on the object's side: a virtual image, which cannot be caught on a screen, and the way a magnifying glass is used[1].

What this setup demonstrates

An Object 15 mm tall stands 150 mm before a thin lens of f = 100 mm. It sends five rays from its tip, spread over the central part of the lens. They cross again 300 mm behind the lens, 30 mm below the axis, on a screen placed there. The Object draws the image it forms, and its inspector reads Image 300 mm after Lens · m = −2.00 · real, inverted, which is what the equation gives: 1/150 + 1/300 = 1/100 and −300/150 = −2. The two rulers mark s and s′.

Things to try, by dragging the Object along the axis (the screen stays where it is, so the rays no longer meet on it):

Change the focal length instead and the same readout follows. The Imaging with lenses calculator plots the image distance and the magnification against the object distance, for this thin lens and for a real, thick one.

What you won't see

The lens is an ideal thin lens: it sends every ray from one point to exactly one point, whatever the ray's height on the lens or its colour. A real singlet does not: rays through its edge focus closer than rays near its centre, and blue focuses closer than red. The spherical aberration and axial colour examples show both with real glass.

The image the Object draws, and its readout, come from a paraxial construction through the lens, not from the drawn rays. With an ideal lens the two agree. Nothing is diffractive: the image has no resolution limit, and the screen records no brightness.
