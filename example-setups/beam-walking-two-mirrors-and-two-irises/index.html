Beam walking: two mirrors and two irises

Putting a laser beam on a line fixed by two irises with two mirrors: each round of two adjustments leaves 0.44 of the error of the round before.

Open in the canvas →

A live trace of the setup above, shown as a picture. Open it in the canvas to take it apart, retune it, and save or export it as your own.

Background

Most optical setups begin with the same task: a laser beam has to run along a given line, for instance the axis of the lenses that follow. A line in the plane of the table is fixed by two points, and two irises mark them. A beam is also fixed by two numbers, a position and a direction, so two adjustments are needed, and one mirror gives only one. Two mirrors in a row give both.

The two adjustments are not independent: turning either mirror moves the beam on both irises. The standard procedure, called beam walking, deals with that by repetition. Turn the first mirror until the beam is centred on the first iris. Turn the second mirror until it is centred on the second iris; it is now a little off the first. Go back to the first mirror, and repeat. Each round leaves a smaller error.

How much smaller follows from the geometry. Call a the distance from mirror 1 to mirror 2, b from mirror 2 to iris 1, and c from iris 1 to iris 2. For small angles, a turn of mirror 1 moves the beam on the two irises in the ratio (a+b) : (a+b+c), and a turn of mirror 2 in the ratio b : (b+c). Working through one round, the error is multiplied by

b (a+b+c) / ((a+b) (b+c)),

which is always less than one. It is small when the first iris is close to the second mirror and the second iris far away. Pair the mirrors and irises the other way round, mirror 2 with iris 1 and mirror 1 with iris 2, and each round multiplies the error by the inverse of that number: the beam walks away instead.

What this setup demonstrates

A 633 nm laser with a 1 mm beam is folded by two mirrors at 45° onto a line through two irises, each open to 2 mm. (An iris is the Slit element: in a top view an opening is a gap.) The distances are a = 100 mm, b = 50 mm and c = 250 mm. As shipped the beam is aligned, and the power meter behind the second iris reads 1.00 mW.

Now misalign it: select the laser and set its rotation to 1°. The beam meets the plate of the first iris and the meter goes dark; with the irises opened wide it would cross the first 5.4 mm and the second 9.8 mm from the centre. Then walk it back by turning the mirrors (the round handle on a selected mirror, or its rotation in the inspector). Done exactly, with each step putting the centre of the beam on the centre of its iris, the traced beam gives:

Each round leaves 0.444 of the error before it, which is 50 × 400 / (150 × 300), the formula above. The walk ends with mirror 1 at 46.30° and mirror 2 at 45.80°; type those two rotations and the meter reads 1.00 mW again. With a 1 mm beam in a 2 mm iris, the light starts to come through once the beam is within 1.5 mm of the centre and all of it passes within 0.5 mm, so the meter is the guide, as it is on the bench.

Then try the wrong pairing from the same start, with the laser at 1° and both mirrors back at 45°: centre the beam on iris 1 with mirror 2, and on iris 2 with mirror 1. After the first step the beam is 23 mm off iris 2, further than the 9.8 mm it started with. In the next step, turning mirror 1 walks the beam off the edge of mirror 2 while it is still about 5 mm from the centre of iris 2.

What you won't see

The setup is a top view, so it shows one of the two planes. On a table each mirror also tilts the beam up and down, and the same walk is done for the height, with the same two mirrors and irises. Height is not part of the drawing.

A mirror here turns about the centre of its face, and the beam is 25 rays with sharp edges, so the meter's reading changes in steps of 40 µW as rays are cut off. A real mount turns about a point behind the mirror, and a real beam has a smooth profile and is partly transmitted by an iris well before it is centred.
