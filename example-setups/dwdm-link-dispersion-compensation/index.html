DWDM link with dispersion compensation

Four 40 GHz pulse trains on the ITU grid share 10 km of fibre: the fibre spreads every pulse past its bit slot, and a short spool of dispersion-compensating fibre gathers them back.

Background

Dense wavelength-division multiplexing (DWDM) is how one optical fibre carries many independent signals: each channel gets its own optical frequency, all of them travel together, and the receiving end separates them again. The frequencies are not chosen freely. ITU-T G.694.1 fixes a grid anchored at 193.1 THz with spacings of 100 GHz (about 0.8 nm near 1550 nm) and narrower, so that lasers, filters and amplifiers from different makers line up[1].

Combining and separating channels that close together needs filters with a passband about a nanometre wide. One classic way is a cascade of thin-film band-pass filters: multilayer interference coatings that pass one channel and reflect everything else[2]. At the receiver the reflected light goes on to the next filter, so each stage drops one channel; run backwards, the same cascade adds channels onto a common fibre.

The fibre itself limits how fast each channel can go. Standard single-mode fibre (ITU-T G.652) loses about 0.2 dB/km near 1550 nm, but there its chromatic dispersion is about 17 ps/(nm·km), a group-velocity dispersion β₂ ≈ −21.7 ps²/km[3,4]. A short pulse contains a band of wavelengths that arrive at different times, so it spreads. For a transform-limited Gaussian pulse of width τ₀ the width after a length L is τ₀·√(1 + (4 ln2·β₂L/τ₀²)²)[4]: the shorter the pulse, the faster it spreads.

At 40 Gb/s the bit slot is 25 ps. Return-to-zero formats with a 33% duty cycle put a pulse of about 8 ps in each slot[5]. After 10 km of standard fibre that pulse is about 73 ps wide and smears across three bit slots. Dispersion that only depends on wavelength can be undone, however: a fibre with dispersion of the opposite sign adds back what the link took away[6]. Dispersion-compensating fibre (DCF) is designed for this, with strongly normal dispersion, so a spool a fraction of the span's length cancels it, at the price of a higher loss per kilometre[7].

What this setup demonstrates

Four pulsed lasers sit on every second channel of the 100 GHz grid (193.4, 193.2, 193.0 and 192.8 THz: 1550.12, 1551.72, 1553.33 and 1554.94 nm). Each emits 1 mW of 8.3 ps transform-limited Gaussian pulses at 40 GHz. A train with a pulse in every slot is the clock of a 40 Gb/s return-to-zero signal, every bit a one, with no data pattern on it. Four band-pass filters, 1.2 nm wide, put the channels on one path. The probe just after the multiplexer shows the four lines 1.6 nm apart.

The link is 10 km of standard fibre (β₂ = −21.7 ps²/km, 0.2 dB/km), then 1.7 km of DCF (β₂ = +127.6 ps²/km, about −100 ps/(nm·km), 0.5 dB/km; representative values, not a specific product). Together the two fibres add a group-delay dispersion of −217 ps² and +216.9 ps², which leaves −0.08 ps². The demultiplexer drops one channel at each filter. Every receiver reads 8.3 ps and 0.52 mW: 0 dBm launched, less 2.0 dB in the standard fibre and 0.85 dB in the DCF. The time probe on RX1 shows four clean pulses in 100 ps.

Things to try. Select the blue DCF and set its β₂ to 0. Every pulse now arrives 73 ps wide and the time trace on RX1 fills in: neighbouring bits overlap. Set the DCF back and shorten it to 1.2 km: 64 ps² of dispersion remain and the pulses read 22.9 ps, nearly the whole bit slot. Tune TX2 up by 0.8 nm, halfway between channels. Its filter now passes only the edge of its spectrum: RX2 receives about 0.07 mW instead of 0.52 mW, in a pulse stretched to 23.6 ps, and the rest is reflected on to the express port. Delete a laser and only its receiver goes dark.

What you won't see

Each laser emits a regular pulse train, not data, so there are no bit patterns, no eye diagram and no bit-error rate. Noise from sources, amplifiers and receivers is not modelled. Neither are fibre nonlinearity (self- and cross-phase modulation, four-wave mixing), polarization-mode dispersion, or the variation of dispersion with wavelength (dispersion slope). Every channel sees the same β₂ here, while in real standard fibre the dispersion at the outer channels differs by a few per cent.

The filters are ideal flat tops: complete transmission inside a 1.2 nm band, complete reflection outside it, and no roll-off, ripple or dispersion of their own. Adjacent-channel leakage therefore stays at zero until a channel is tuned into a neighbouring passband. The fibres are characterised by a fixed loss per metre and a constant β₂. Coupling at their ends is geometric and does not compute mode-field overlap.
