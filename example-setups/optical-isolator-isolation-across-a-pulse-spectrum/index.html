Optical isolator across a pulse spectrum

An isolator turns light by exactly 45° at one wavelength only: a short pulse, tens of nanometres wide, leaks back at the wings of its spectrum.

Background

An optical isolator lets light through one way and blocks it the other. It is an input polarizer, a Faraday rotator that turns the polarization by 45°, and an output polarizer at 45° to the first. Going forward the light arrives aligned with the output polarizer; coming back it is turned a further 45° in the same sense and reaches the input polarizer crossed, because the Faraday rotation is fixed by the magnet's field, not by the direction the light travels[1].

The rotation is the Verdet constant of the rotator's material times the field times the length, and the Verdet constant depends on wavelength: in terbium gallium garnet (TGG) it falls roughly as the inverse square of the wavelength[2]. An isolator is therefore built for one wavelength. Away from it the turn is wrong by an angle δ, the forward transmission falls as cos²δ and the backward leakage rises as sin²δ. A narrow laser line barely notices; a femtosecond pulse, whose spectrum is tens of nanometres wide, does.

What this setup demonstrates

A Ti:sapphire laser sends 1 W of 15 fs pulses at 800 nm, a spectrum about 63 nm wide, through a 90:10 pick-off and an optical isolator set to its physical model: TGG, designed for 800 nm, with polarizers of 40 dB extinction. A mirror sends the beam back. What the isolator lets through on the way back is picked off onto a spectrometer.

The isolator's inspector gives its figures: a field-length product of 0.0106 T·m makes the turn 45° at 800 nm, where the isolation is the polarizers' 40.0 dB; at 768.6 and 831.4 nm, half the pulse's bandwidth either side, it is only 22.0 and 23.2 dB. The spectrometer reads 0.352 mW, and its spectrum is two humps, at 761 and 836 nm, with a hole at 800 nm: the light that comes back is the light whose wavelength the isolator was not built for.

The last line is the isolator at its best: 1 W × 0.9 × 0.1 × 10⁻⁴, the 40 dB of its polarizers. The 15 fs pulse returns 39 times more.

What you won't see

The isolator's rotator uses the Verdet constant of one TGG sample at room temperature, measured between 500 and 1100 nm; other samples read up to about 19 % higher, which moves the figures away from the design wavelength. Each wavelength of the spectrum is weighed on its own, but the polarizers are ideal apart from their extinction, which is the same at every wavelength, and absorption, thermal effects at high power, the rotator's dispersion of the pulse and the pick-off's own polarization dependence are not modelled. The light that the isolator lets back and the pick-off does not take ends at the laser: there is no feedback into it.
