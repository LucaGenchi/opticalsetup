Retinal imaging with a Linnik interferometer

In an ophthalmic interferometer the eye is the sample-arm objective: its own optics focus the light, the retina sends some of it back, and a lens with a mirror reproduces the same arrangement in the reference arm.

Background

Optical coherence tomography (OCT) images the layers of the retina by interfering light returned from the eye with light from a reference mirror. In full-field OCT the whole field is illuminated at once and imaged onto a fast camera instead of scanning a single spot. A Linnik interferometer is a Michelson interferometer with an objective lens in each arm; for retinal imaging, the sample-arm objective is the eye itself, so the reference arm carries a lens of similar focal length and a mirror at its focus to match it[1].

The same lens-and-mirror pair is also how such instruments are tested without a volunteer: Auksorius, Borycki and Wojtkowski chose a 2.5 cm lens in front of a resolution target as a close optical approximation of a human eye, and an eye focal length of 2.5 cm in their system estimates[1]. Their system then destroyed the spatial coherence of the swept laser with a fast deformable membrane, so that multiply scattered light from the retina no longer interferes and corrupts the image with crosstalk.

What this setup demonstrates

The layout is a simplified version of the paper's Fig. 1, with the deformable membrane switched off (a flat mirror, left out here) and the relay lenses omitted. An 840 nm source splits at a 50/50 beamsplitter. The sample arm is a Human eye with a 25 mm focal length, a 6 mm dilated pupil and 5 % retinal reflectivity: the collimated beam focuses on the retina, and the reflected part leaves the pupil collimated again. The reference arm is the same thing built from parts — a 25 mm lens with a mirror at its focus — behind a neutral density filter that leaves about 6 % after the double pass, as in the paper. Both returns recombine at the beamsplitter and a tube lens focuses them onto the camera.

Change the eye's Retinal reflectivity to see the sample return and the camera reading change, or its Lens focal length to mimic a refractive error: the return then leaves the eye converging or diverging instead of collimated.

What you won't see

The retina is a flat specular mirror, not a layered, diffusely scattering fundus, so the scene has no depth structure to image and no crosstalk to remove. The source is a single fixed wavelength rather than a sweep, and the carrier phase through the eye is not modeled, so the camera adds the two returns' powers instead of forming OCT fringes. The deformable membrane, its spatial-coherence gating, and the paper's three-lens defocus compensation are not modeled.
