Optical sectioning: linear versus nonlinear signals

Two thin fluorescent layers on one focused beam: under one-photon excitation both glow, under two-photon excitation only the one at the focus does.

Background

A focused beam carries the same power through every plane along its path; only the area it is spread over changes. A signal that grows in proportion to the intensity — one-photon fluorescence, spontaneous Raman — therefore comes from a thin layer equally well wherever that layer sits in the cone: out-of-focus planes add their light to the image, and a confocal microscope needs a pinhole to reject it.

A signal that grows as the n-th power of the intensity behaves differently. Through a layer the power is fixed, but the intensity falls as the beam widens, so the signal falls as the beam area to the power n − 1. For a Gaussian focus that is (1 + (z/zR)²)−(n−1) at a distance z from the focus, with zR the Rayleigh range. Two-photon fluorescence (n = 2) from a layer a few Rayleigh ranges away is already a small fraction of its value at the focus; three-photon fluorescence and third-harmonic generation (n = 3) fall faster still. That is the optical sectioning of multiphoton microscopy: the signal itself only comes from the focal region, so no pinhole is needed.

What this setup demonstrates

Each arm focuses a 10 mm beam with an f = 100 mm lens through two thin GFP layers: one 40 mm before the focus, one at it. In the upper arm a 488 nm laser drives one-photon fluorescence and both layers glow. In the lower arm a 920 nm femtosecond laser drives two-photon fluorescence; its layers have Focus-dependent yield switched on, and only the layer at the focus glows.

Select a lower layer to read where the focus is: the layer at the focus reads a factor of 1, the other one about 10−5, with a Rayleigh range of about 120 µm for this beam. Move the focal layer by some tens of micrometres to watch its yield fall, or switch Focus-dependent yield off to see what the workbench draws without it: both layers glowing alike.

What you won't see

The layers are thin sheets, so the sectioning shown is the thin-layer response, not the signal a thick specimen would build up along the whole beam. The focus is an ideal diffraction-limited Gaussian in air, estimated from the convergence of the traced rays: no aberrations, refractive index, scattering or high-NA vector effects. The yield is relative to the same layer at the focus; the absolute brightness of each arm is set by its source power and the layer's signal efficiency, not by a two-photon cross-section or peak intensity.
