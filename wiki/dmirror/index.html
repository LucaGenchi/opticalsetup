D-shaped mirror

A flat mirror coated to one sharp edge, so one beam passes beside it while another, millimetres away, is reflected: the pickoff of folded compressors.

Open in the canvas →

A live trace, not a picture of one — but this preview is not interactive. Open it in the canvas to move things, change parameters, and save or export your own version.

In the real world

A round mirror cannot sit close to a beam: its mount, its bevel and the last millimetre of uncoated glass all get in the way. A D-shaped mirror is cut along a chord and coated right up to that straight edge, which is left sharp rather than bevelled. Two beams a few millimetres apart can then be separated: one passes just beside the edge, the other lands on the coating next to it and is reflected away.

That is the job it does in a prism or grating compressor. The beam goes in past the edge, through the dispersive elements to a fold mirror that sends it back a little displaced, through the elements again, and onto the D-shaped mirror, which picks the compressed beam off. In the lab the displacement is usually vertical: the incoming beam passes just above the mirror and the returning one, a few millimetres lower, is reflected out.

In OpticalSetup

The canvas is a top view, so the D-shaped mirror reflects exactly as a flat mirror does, with the same reflectivity settings. Its drawing marks which end is the sharp, coated edge (square, with a short bar) and which is the round back; Sharp edge flips it.

Because the view is two-dimensional, the pickoff works in the plane: the returning beam has to come back displaced sideways and pass the mirror's edge on the other side from the incoming one. A vertical displacement, the usual lab arrangement, is not represented. For a dispersive double pass, displace the return with a slightly tilted flat end mirror rather than with a retroreflector: an in-plane roof reflector reverses the colours' order across the beam, so the second pass doubles the spatial chirp instead of undoing it.

Simplified vs. reality

Only the reflecting face is traced. The substrate, the uncoated back and the mount do not block light, and the edge is perfectly sharp: real D-shaped mirrors clip a beam that comes within a few hundred micrometres of the edge and scatter a little from it. Reflectivity has the plain mirror's caveats: no angle, polarization or wavelength dependence.
