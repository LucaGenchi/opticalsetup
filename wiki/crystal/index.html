Crystal

Converts a chosen fraction of incident light into harmonic, parametric, supercontinuum, or custom output, with an option to retain the residual pump.

In the real world

Nonlinear optical crystals can generate light at frequencies different from the input. For example, second-harmonic generation doubles frequency and halves vacuum wavelength. Efficient conversion depends on excitation intensity and phase matching, rather than a freely chosen conversion percentage[1].

Optical parametric oscillators

In an optical parametric oscillator (OPO) the process runs the other way: inside a χ⁽²⁾ crystal each pump photon splits into two lower-energy photons, the signal and the idler. By convention the signal is the shorter of the two. Placing the crystal in a cavity that feeds one of them back turns this weak parametric gain into a tunable coherent source for spectral ranges that direct laser emission covers poorly or not at all[2,3].

Energy conservation: one pump photon becomes one signal and one idler photon.

Manley–Rowe: signal and idler are generated with equal photon fluxes, so the generated powers divide in proportion to photon energy.

Because the photon fluxes are equal, the longer-wavelength output is always generated with the smaller share of the power; what finally leaves a resonator also depends on its output coupling and losses. Pumped at 532 nm with an 800 nm signal, the idler lies at 1588 nm and receives about a third of the converted power. The penalty grows with the wavelength ratio: producing 5 µm light from a 1 µm pump can use at most about 20 % of the pump power[4].

Threshold. Like a laser, an OPO oscillates only once the round-trip parametric gain exceeds the cavity losses. Unlike a laser, the crystal stores no energy: gain exists only while the pump is present. In a singly resonant OPO, where only the signal is fed back, plane-wave theory predicts complete pump depletion at (π/2)² ≈ 2.5 times threshold. Driven harder, signal and idler start converting back into pump light, which is why the efficiency saturates[4].

Pump depletion D of a plane-wave singly resonant OPO at N times threshold: zero at N = 1, complete at N = (π/2)².

Linewidth. The frequencies must still add up, so the width of the pump spectrum has to go somewhere. The cavity holds the resonant wave, and the non-resonant wave takes up whatever the pump and resonant wave leave. Pump frequency fluctuations that the resonant wave does not follow appear on the non-resonant one, so imposing them on the resonant wave is a way to remove them from the other[5]. This page and OpticalSetup call the resonant wave the signal; real OPOs resonate either. If pump and resonant wave fluctuate independently and both spectra are Gaussian, the non-resonant wave's frequency width is the quadrature sum of theirs; correlated fluctuations change that, which is why an idler can be narrower than its pump.

Common operating regimes:

  • Synchronously pumped fs and ps OPOs. The cavity round trip is matched to the repetition period of a mode-locked pump, so each signal pulse is amplified by the next pump pulse. The output pulse trains stay locked to the pump's. A Ti:sapphire-pumped fs OPO delivers typically 200 fs signal pulses tunable from 1000 to 1600 nm, with idler from 1750 to 4000 nm, at 80 MHz[6]. A picosecond system for coherent Raman microscopy delivers 2 ps pulses whose signal has the same 10 cm⁻¹ spectral width as its 1032 nm beam[7].
  • Nanosecond OPOs pumped by Q-switched lasers are the high-energy workhorses. Their linewidth is set mainly by the crystal's phase-matching bandwidth and the cavity rather than by the pump: a 355 nm-pumped system tunes a signal from 410 to 710 nm and an idler from 710 to 2600 nm in 3–5 ns pulses, with a linewidth below 5 cm⁻¹[8]. The oscillation needs time to build up from noise during each pump pulse, which raises the threshold[4] and makes the output pulses somewhat shorter than the pump's[2].
  • Continuous-wave singly resonant OPOs pass the pump frequency fluctuations that the resonant wave does not follow on to the non-resonant wave[5].

In OpticalSetup

Choose a conversion mode before expecting an output: the default None passes light unchanged. SHG and THG set the wavelength to one half or one third of the input. Custom output uses the entered wavelength; supercontinuum creates a fixed illustrative band. Conversion efficiency sets the converted ray weight, and residual-pump transmission retains the unconverted branch. The example doubles 1064 nm to 532 nm and separates it from the remaining pump with a dichroic.

OPO mode models a singly resonant oscillator phenomenologically. Set the pump wavelength the crystal is phase-matched for, its acceptance window, and the resonant signal wavelength; the idler is shown as a readout. Pump light converts when its centre lies inside the acceptance window, whatever its bandwidth. The signal stays where the cavity holds it and the idler follows the arriving pump by energy conservation. Light is never converted twice by the same crystal, even after passing through another one; a different crystal can still convert it. The conversion efficiency is a fixed fraction of the pump, divided between signal and idler by the lossless Manley–Rowe split.

Linewidths are handled as FWHM in wavenumber. Signal as wide as the pump is a heuristic for synchronously pumped fs and ps OPOs; Signal width set suits ns and CW OPOs, where the cavity sets it; in both, the idler is derived as the uncorrelated Gaussian sum. Signal and idler widths set takes both from a datasheet. A narrow output is a Gaussian in wavelength; one wider than 1 % of its frequency is sampled from its Gaussian in wavenumber, so it is correctly skewed toward long wavelengths. Dichroics, filters and spectrometers downstream act on these new spectra rather than on the pump's. At exactly twice the pump wavelength, signal and idler with equal widths form one degenerate beam; with different widths they stay two coincident beams, each with its own spectrum.

Pulses. Signal and idler are pulse trains of their own, synchronised to the pump: they keep its repetition rate, arrival timing and modulation gates, so a detector sees a non-degenerate pump, signal and idler as three separate trains. Their duration is the pump duration times an authored factor, raised to the transform limit of the output's Gaussian spectrum if that is longer, and their spectral phase is treated as unknown. Choose Transform-limited to set each duration from its bandwidth instead; an output with zero linewidth is an idealised monochromatic pulse train, like a pulsed source set to 0 nm, and cannot be transform-limited. Group delay dispersion is counted from the crystal exit. The inspector lists each output's linewidth and duration.

Simplified vs. reality

Conversion is a wavelength-and-power proxy, and OPO mode is a phenomenological model rather than a cavity simulation. Phase matching is not calculated from material data: the signal wavelength and acceptance window are authored, and crystal choice or temperature do not affect them. There is no threshold, gain or pump depletion: the authored efficiency converts the same fraction at any pump power. Cavity round trips, synchronisation to the repetition rate, build-up time, output coupling, group-velocity walk-off and spatial mode overlap are not traced. Output durations are authored rather than predicted and carry no spectral phase. Pump spectra are reduced to a Gaussian of the same FWHM, and the idler width assumes uncorrelated Gaussian fluctuations unless both widths are set.
