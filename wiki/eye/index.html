Human eye

A reduced eye: a pupil and one lens focus light onto a retina that detects it and can reflect part of it back, mirror-like or diffusely.

In the real world

The eye forms an image on the retina using the refractive power of the cornea and crystalline lens, about 60 dioptres in total. The iris sets the pupil, roughly 2–4 mm in daylight and up to about 8 mm when dilated. An eye whose power does not match its length is myopic (too strong) or hyperopic (too weak), and the spectacle power that corrects it is its refractive error. The fundus sends a small fraction of the light back, mostly as diffuse scattering from several layers; this returned light is what ophthalmoscopes, fundus cameras, scanning laser ophthalmoscopes and OCT systems detect.

In OpticalSetup

OpticalSetup models a reduced eye in air: the pupil clips incoming rays, one thin lens at the pupil bends them, and the retina sits one eye length behind it, where a retinal detector records the signal. A new eye is the textbook 60 D eye: 16.7 mm focal length, the same length, and a 4 mm pupil. Its length is the air-equivalent one, shorter than the 24 mm of a real eyeball, so that ray angles inside and outside the eye stay right.

Set the power as a focal length or in dioptres: both edit the same lens, and a sketch stores the focal length whichever you choose. The Refractive error line compares the power with the length; change either to model a myopic or hyperopic eye and watch the retinal spot spread.

Retinal reflectivity (0–100 %, default 0) sends that fraction of the light back from the retina. Diffuse share splits it: the mirror-like part goes back through the lens, so a collimated beam focused on the retina returns collimated, like a cat's eye, and the eye can stand in for the lens-and-mirror pair that ophthalmic instruments use to model it. The diffuse part scatters in all directions as a Lambertian surface would. Only the part headed for the pupil can leave, so it is traced as a small fan across the pupil carrying exactly that share; what would hit the eye wall is absorbed. A beamsplitter in front of the eye routes either return to a camera or detector.

The retinal reading counts all the light that reaches the retina, whatever fraction is reflected, and the inspector shows it in watts, carried from the sources' configured power like a power meter's reading. Light arriving at the back of the eyeball is blocked by the eye wall: it neither reaches the retina nor reflects. Sketches saved before the 60 D defaults open with the 30 mm eye, 12 mm pupil and 30 mm focal length they were drawn with.

Simplified vs. reality

This is a geometric teaching model, not an anatomical eye prescription. One thin lens replaces the cornea and crystalline lens, so the pupil and the lens coincide: an instrument that images a source exactly into the pupil (Maxwellian view) is approximated, not reproduced. It does not predict visual acuity, diffraction, aberrations, accommodation dynamics or retinal physiology.

The retina is a single plane. Its diffuse share uses the 3-D Lambertian fraction for a round pupil, exact on axis and estimated from the in-plane extent off axis; there are no retinal layers, no depth structure and no wavelength dependence. The power at the retina is not compared with any exposure limit and must not be used for laser-safety decisions. The carrier phase through the eye is not modeled, so the eye's return adds power to an interferometer's output but does not form fringes.
