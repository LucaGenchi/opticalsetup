Faraday rotator

A magneto-optic rod in an axial field that turns polarization by V·B·L, in a sense set by the field, so a return pass doubles the turn.

In the real world

A Faraday rotator turns the plane of polarization of light that crosses it. It is a rod of a magneto-optic material, often terbium gallium garnet (TGG) in the visible and near infrared, inside a strong magnet whose field runs along the beam. The angle is the Verdet constant of the material times the field times the length of the rod[1], and it changes with wavelength: for TGG it falls roughly as the inverse square of the wavelength across the near infrared, from about 131 rad/(T·m) at 632 nm to 37.5 rad/(T·m) at 1064 nm in one measured sample[2].

What sets it apart from a waveplate is that it is not reciprocal. The polarization turns in a sense fixed by the magnetic field, not by the direction the light travels: light sent back through the rod is turned the same way about the field again, so a double pass doubles the rotation instead of undoing it. Every other element in this category is reciprocal, and no combination of them can do this. It is what makes an optical isolator possible, and the Faraday mirror: a 45° rotator in front of a mirror returns light polarized at 90° to how it arrived, even after it has crossed an unknown birefringent element on the way in and out[3].

In OpticalSetup

Choose the Material (TGG, or a custom Verdet constant), the field B, its direction (along or against the element's arrow) and the length L. The element turns the polarization of the light that crosses it by θ = V·B·L, and the inspector gives the turn at each wavelength present, for each direction of travel. A positive V turns the polarization anticlockwise, seen looking towards the source, for light travelling along the field, and clockwise for light travelling against it; in the app's polarization angle (measured from the drawing plane towards you) light along the field has its angle raised by V·B·L. TGG's V is negative. Light crossing the rod against the field therefore sees its angle change the opposite way in its own direction of travel, the same turn about the field, and a return pass doubles the rotation.

To first order the turn does not depend on the angle at which light crosses the rod: the path through it grows as 1/cos while the field along the ray falls as cos. A beam's power is unchanged.

To build a Faraday mirror, put a polarizing beamsplitter, any waveplate, a 45° rotator and a mirror in a line: whatever the waveplate's angle, the light that comes back leaves through the beamsplitter's other port. The example Faraday mirror shows it. The optical isolator has a model built from polarizers and this rotator.

Simplified vs. reality

The rod is drawn to its length but acts as a thin element at its centre: its index, its path delay and dispersion, absorption, surface reflections and heating at high power are not modelled, nor is a field that varies along the rod. TGG's Verdet constant is that of one sample at room temperature between 500 and 1100 nm; other samples read up to about 19 % higher[2]. Outside that range the element computes no rotation: the light keeps the polarization it arrived with, and the readings past it say so. A custom Verdet constant is the same at every wavelength. Each wavelength of a broadband beam turns by its own amount, and the beam carries on as their average, partly polarized where they differ: that gives the power a polarizer passes after it but not the spectrum it leaves, and the readings past it say so. A second Faraday rotator or isolator anywhere after it, even past a polarizer, would need each wavelength's own state and share, which the average has lost: its result is declared not computed. Part of a band outside the material's data keeps its state, and the readings name that part.
